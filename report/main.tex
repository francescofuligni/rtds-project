\begin{abstract}
Released machine learning models can leak the data they were trained on. We study this risk for \emph{Rashomon sets} of decision trees (TreeFARMS), where an attacker can draw on several near-optimal trees instead of one, and ask two questions prior work leaves open: how much does an attacker's own background knowledge of auxiliary attributes change reconstruction success, and can an individual reconstructed record be given a validated confidence value rather than only an aggregate error rate? Using a constraint-programming reconstruction attack on a curated subset of the UCI Adult dataset, we find that background knowledge of marital-status and/or education significantly reduces reconstruction error on sex ($A{+}B < A \approx B < \text{None}$, $p<0.05$ after Holm-Bonferroni correction for the large majority of tested conditions), while a hypothesized structural mechanism for this effect (trees jointly splitting on the sensitive and known attributes) turns out to be statistically real but practically negligible. We then build a per-row confidence signal from solver agreement across repeated re-solves and calibrate it into an actual validated probability via isotonic regression on a held-out seed split, cutting Brier score by roughly a quarter relative to using raw agreement as a probability and reducing expected calibration error by over an order of magnitude. To our knowledge this is the first calibrated, held-out-validated per-record confidence measure proposed for reconstruction attacks against an ensemble of released models.
\end{abstract}
