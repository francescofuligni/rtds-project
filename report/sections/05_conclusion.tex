\section{Conclusion}
% Reflect on the contribution, limitations and future work.

Together, the two studies show that average reconstruction error gives an incomplete
picture of privacy leakage from a Rashomon release. Background knowledge helps, but its
value depends on which attributes are known and how they are combined. In our experiments,
the number and selection of released trees have larger effects. Reconstructions guided by public
data also show that recovery is uneven: an attacker can distinguish more
reliable record claims even when reconstruction of the whole dataset remains imperfect.
Release policies should therefore consider both the information contributed by the
selected trees and the reliability of individual reconstructed claims, rather than relying
on average error alone.

\paragraph{Limitations.} Our results come from small samples of the Adult data with a coarse
  binary encoding, known income labels and public data from the same population. Only 640
  different records are possible, and public statistics alone already get 58.5\% of the
  claims right. Correct claims can therefore reflect common population profiles, making
  improvement over this control important when interpreting leakage. The SQ1 controls do
  not use the known A/B attributes, so they do not isolate what the trees add beyond that
  knowledge. The confidence study uses
  20 seeds and no background knowledge, and the solver does not always prove that the returned tables are the
  closest ones, although doubling the solve time and workers on ten seeds changed accuracy by
  only 0.1 points. We chose the public-statistics control's smoothing
  after inspecting its calibration, which favours that control. Finally, we could not confirm that the current data
  cache matches the one used for the original runs.

\paragraph{Future work.} Future work should combine background knowledge with record-level
  confidence, giving the same background information to the control without trees. This
  would test whether known attributes improve the reliability of reconstructed claims.
  Further experiments could use larger tables, finer feature encodings and public data
  from different populations. Extending reconstruction to differentially private leaf
  counts would also allow us to study how noisy releases affect both recovery and confidence.
