\section{Conclusion}
% Reflect on the contribution, limitations and future work.

Together, the two studies show that average reconstruction error gives an incomplete
picture of privacy leakage from a Rashomon release. Background knowledge helps, but its
value depends on which attributes are known and how they are combined. In our experiments,
the number and selection of released trees have larger effects. Reconstructions guided by public data reveal uneven recovery: attackers can distinguish
reliable claims despite imperfect overall reconstruction.
Release policies should consider the selected trees' information and individual claim
reliability, beyond average error.

\paragraph{Limitations.} Our results come from small samples of the Adult data with a coarse
  binary encoding, known income labels and public data from the same population. Only 640
  different records are possible, and public statistics alone already get 58.5\% of the
  claims right. Correct claims can reflect common profiles, so leakage must be assessed against
  this control. The SQ1 controls do
  not use the known A/B attributes, so they do not isolate what the trees add beyond that
  knowledge. The confidence study uses 20 seeds and no background knowledge. Solver optimality
  is not always proven, although doubling solve time and workers on ten seeds changed
  accuracy by only 0.1 points. We chose the public-statistics control's smoothing
  after inspecting its calibration, which favours that control. Finally, we could not confirm that the current data
  cache matches the one used for the original runs.

\paragraph{Future work.} Combining background knowledge with record-level confidence, while giving the
  no-tree control the same knowledge, would test whether known attributes improve claim reliability.
  Further experiments could use larger tables, finer feature encodings and public data
  from different populations. Differentially private leaf counts would let us study how noise affects recovery and confidence.
