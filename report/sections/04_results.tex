\subsection{More trees, and a little background knowledge (SQ1)}
\label{sec:knowledge}
Releasing more trees has a much larger effect than any background attribute we tested
(Table~\ref{tab:attack}). Without background knowledge, the error falls from 34.6\% with one
tree to 25.3\% with 200 trees. With a single tree, the reconstruction is little more than a
default guess: the tree constrains few cells, and the solver tends to fill the free sex
cells with 0, which encodes male. For half of the seeds, the error with one tree equals
exactly the share of women in the sample. Guessing sex at random or from its public frequency gives
50.4\% and 44.5\% error. These controls are weak, which shows how much the choice of baseline
affects how strong an attack looks.

Knowing marital status together with a second attribute (A+B) helps most, but only by about
one percentage point. Education on its own helps more than age, and the gain from age alone
is not significant. Averaged over all release sizes the gains survive Holm correction, but
none of the tests at a single release size does, so we interpret them with caution.

One reason is that the solver adds noise of about the same size. The condition without
background knowledge is the same problem in both runs, yet for 15\% of the (seed, $m$) pairs
the two runs gave a different error (standard deviation 2.0 points), because the solver
accepts any consistent table and may find a different one each time. We also found no link between the gains and how often trees split
on both sex and the known attribute.

\subsection{Which trees are released (SQ2)}
\label{sec:selection}
\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.44\linewidth]{attack_selection_comparison.png}
  \caption{Sex reconstruction error with marital status and education known (A+B), for
      randomly chosen trees and for the first trees in TreeFARMS order. Means over 94--100 seeds;

  \label{fig:selection}

Which trees are released matters more than background knowledge. With marital status and
education known, the error at $m=200$ is 24.3\% when the trees are chosen at random, against
30.4\% when they are the first 200 trees in TreeFARMS order (Figure~\ref{fig:selection},
paired $p=8\times10^{-11}$). The two curves separate after about five trees.  This
experiment does not show which property of the selected trees causes the difference.

\subsection{Record-level confidence and correctness (SQ3)}
\paragraph{Accuracy and average confidence.} With 2,000 public records, prior-guided sampling
  gets more claims right than public statistics alone at every number of trees we tested
  (paired $p\leq2\times10^{-4}$; Table~\ref{tab:confidence}), and its average confidence is
  only 0.7 to 2.3 percentage points above its accuracy. At $m=50$ this also roughly holds within each range of
  confidence (Figure~\ref{fig:confidence}, left), although that does not prove calibration.

\paragraph{Correct records are concentrated among high-confidence claims.} At $m=50$, 45\% of the claims reach a confidence of at least 0.9, and
  on average 97.6\% of these are correct, while public statistics alone give only about 10\%
  of the claims such a high confidence. The attacker can thus point out a large group of
  almost certainly correct records, which the overall accuracy of 77.8\% does not show,
  although it does not know who these people are. Still, claims found
  in all 20 tables were wrong in 2 of 673 cases. 

\paragraph{Small public samples.} With only 200 public records, the plain version becomes
  overconfident (Figure~\ref{fig:confidence}, right): at $m=10$ its average confidence is 8.6
  percentage points above its accuracy. The Dirichlet variant brings this down to 1.6 points.
  The improvement is significant at all three tested release sizes (paired
  $p\leq3\times10^{-5}$ for the absolute gap), and accuracy does not change measurably. The
  Dirichlet version gives fewer claims a confidence of 0.9 or more, but more of those are
  correct.  With 2,000 public records, the Dirichlet prior has no measurable effect on accuracy, ranking or the confidence gap ($p\geq0.13$).
