\subsection{More trees, and a little background knowledge (SQ1)}
\label{sec:knowledge}
\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.495\linewidth]{attack_marital_education.png}\hfill
  \includegraphics[width=0.495\linewidth]{attack_marital_age.png}
  \caption{Sex reconstruction error under random tree selection, mean over 93--100
      valid seeds per condition and release size. Both panels compare None, A, B and
      A+B, with A = marital status. Left: B = education. Right: B = age.}
  \label{fig:attack}
\end{figure}

Releasing more trees has a much larger effect than any background attribute we tested
(Figure~\ref{fig:attack} and Table~\ref{tab:attack}). Without background knowledge, the error falls from 34.6\% with one
tree to 25.3\% with 200 trees. Each additional tree imposes path and leaf-count constraints on the same sample,
which may rule out previously compatible tables. Guessing sex at random
or from its public frequency gives 50.4\% and 44.5\% error. These comparisons show an
advantage over random and public-frequency guesses, but do not isolate the contribution
of the trees when background attributes are known.

Knowing an attribute fixes cells that would otherwise remain unknown, reducing the set
of tables consistent with the release. Background knowledge modestly improves sex
reconstruction, but the benefit depends on
which attributes are known. Marital status and education alone each reduce
error by about 0.5 percentage points on average, while age alone gives a smaller,
non-significant gain of 0.1 points. Knowing marital status together with education or age
gives the largest average reductions: 1.3 and 0.9 points, respectively. These results
suggest that reconstruction depends on which attributes are known and how they are
combined, not only on their number.

\subsection{Which trees are released (SQ2)}
\label{sec:selection}
\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.44\linewidth]{attack_selection_comparison.png}
  \caption{Sex reconstruction error with marital status and education known (A+B), for
      randomly chosen trees and for the first trees in TreeFARMS order. Means over 94--100 seeds;
      bands show $\pm$1.96 standard errors.}
  \label{fig:selection}
\end{figure}

In our experiment, tree selection has a larger effect on sex reconstruction than the
tested background attributes. With marital status and education known, random selection
gives 24.3\% error at $m=200$, compared with 30.4\% for the first 200 trees in TreeFARMS
order---a difference of 6.2 percentage points (Figure~\ref{fig:selection}, paired
$p=8\times10^{-11}$). The curves begin to separate after about five trees. A plausible
explanation lies in TreeFARMS' enumeration order: consecutive trees can share a root split
and entire branches because they are extracted from grouped combinations of subtrees.
Sampling across the Rashomon set may therefore provide less redundant reconstruction
constraints, allowing the attacker to recover more information from the same number of
released trees.

\subsection{Record-level confidence and correctness (SQ3)}
\paragraph{Accuracy and average confidence.} With 2,000 public records, prior-guided sampling
  gets more claims right than public statistics alone at every number of trees we tested
  (paired $p\leq2\times10^{-4}$; Table~\ref{tab:confidence}), and its average confidence is
  only 0.7 to 2.3 percentage points above its accuracy. At $m=50$, confidence and accuracy are also roughly aligned within each confidence
  range (Figure~\ref{fig:confidence}, left), although that does not prove calibration.

\paragraph{Correct records are concentrated among high-confidence claims.} At $m=50$, 45\% of the claims reach a confidence of at least 0.9, and
  on average 97.6\% of these are correct, while public statistics alone give only about 10\%
  of the claims such a high confidence. Overall accuracy hides this concentration of correct claims. The claims do not identify
  specific people.
  Even claims found in all 20 tables were wrong in 2 of 673 cases.

\paragraph{Small public samples.} With only 200 public records, the plain version becomes
  overconfident (Figure~\ref{fig:confidence}, right): at $m=10$ its average confidence is 8.6
  percentage points above its accuracy. Treating uncertain public frequencies as fixed can overstate agreement. The Dirichlet
  variant represents this uncertainty by varying frequencies between reference tables,
  reducing the average gap to 1.6 points.
  The improvement is significant at all three tested release sizes (paired
  $p\leq3\times10^{-5}$ for the absolute gap), and accuracy does not change measurably. Fewer claims reach a confidence of at least 0.9, but their precision improves. With 2,000 public
  records, no significant difference was detected in accuracy, ranking or the confidence
  gap ($p\geq0.13$).
