\subsection{More trees, and a little background knowledge (SQ1)}
\label{sec:knowledge}
Figure~\ref{fig:attack} compares the education and age background-knowledge runs.
The number of released trees drives the leakage in both panels
(Table~\ref{tab:attack}). In the education run, without background knowledge,
the error falls from 34.6\% at
$m=1$ to 25.3\% at $m=200$ ($-9.4$ points, $p=2\times10^{-11}$). At $m=1$ the attack is
no better than always guessing male: a single tree constrains few cells, and the solver
tends to fill the free sex cells with 0, which encodes male. For half of the seeds the
$m=1$ error equals the share of women in the sample exactly. Against the majority guess
it gains 7.7 points at $m=200$ ($p=5\times10^{-11}$). The uniform and frequency
baselines would make it look strong from the start.

Background knowledge reduces the error by about one additional point. Averaged over $m$, marital status with
education lowers the error by 1.3 points, and with age by 0.9 points. Single attributes
reduce error by at most 0.5 points, and age alone gives no measurable improvement. In the age
panel, error at $m=200$ is 25.1\% with age alone and 23.6\% with marital status
and age known. These values describe $m=200$, while Gain averages the improvement over all $m$.
As a single known
attribute, education gives 0.5 points more gain than age ($p=0.03$,
uncorrected). Tested at each $m$ separately, none of the 42 tests per run survives Holm
correction. Two further checks help interpret these small effects. First, variation between solver runs
is similar in size to these gains: None is the same problem in both runs, yet 15\% of its
(seed, $m$) cells returned a different error (s.d.\ 2.0 points), because CP-SAT's
parallel workers race and any consistent table is accepted. Second, the mechanism we
expected, that knowing A helps when trees split on sex and A on the same path, is not
supported: the share of trees with both splits does not predict the gain ($|r|<0.04$, all
$p>0.2$).

The choice of trees matters more than background knowledge
(Figure~\ref{fig:selection}). With marital status and
education known, random releases reach 24.3\% error at $m=200$, against 30.4\% for the
first 200 trees in enumeration order ($-6.2$ points, $p=8\times10^{-11}$), and the
curves separate from about $m=10$ ($-2.8$ points, $p=1\times10^{-4}$).
% AUDIT: no saved trees or analysis artifact was found for the following three-seed Jaccard check.
In a small check
on three seeds, the first 10 trees shared 21--33\% of their leaves pairwise (Jaccard
index) and had 21--24 distinct leaves, against 3--4\% and 53--83 for 10 random trees:
the consecutive trees in this check are much more similar to one another. The confidence attack shows the same effect: at
$m=25$ its accuracy is 61.7\% with the first trees and 73.9\% with random ones
($p=9\times10^{-5}$, 20 seeds).

\subsection{Record-level confidence and correctness (SQ3)}
\paragraph{Frequency alone gives unreliable confidence.} Scoring a record by how often
  it appears gives unreliable scores in these experiments. At $m=10$, the share of single-tree reconstructions containing a
  record gives claims that are 6.6\% correct at a mean stated confidence of 49\%.
  Agreement across solver seeds gives 34\% correct at 86\% stated. The reconstructions
  agree because CP-SAT fills free cells the same way, not because the release forces
  those values.

\paragraph{Mean confidence is close to observed accuracy.} Its stated confidence
  exceeds its accuracy by only 0.7 to 2.3 points (Table~\ref{tab:confidence},
  Figure~\ref{fig:confidence} left). Its per-seed calibration error
  $|\text{confidence}-\text{accuracy}|$ matches that of the public-statistics baseline
  ($p\geq0.17$), except at $m=50$, where it is smaller ($p=0.02$). It is also more accurate, 69.2\% at $m=10$
  and 77.8\% at $m=50$ against 58.5\% without trees ($p\leq2\times10^{-4}$ at every $m$).
  All 20 seeds improve at $m=25$ and 50. It also ranks claims better (AUROC 0.79--0.83
  against 0.74). These small average gaps do not show that every confidence level is calibrated.

\paragraph{High-confidence claims account for much of the recovery.} At $m=50$ the
  high-confidence claims cover 45\% of the rows, with mean per-seed precision of
  97.6\%. Public statistics alone cover 10\%. Claims at confidence 1.0 (all 20 samples agree) were
  wrong in 2 of 673 cases. The overall accuracy of 78\% hides this concentration: about half of the
  claims receive high confidence, with mean per-seed precision of 97.6\%.

\paragraph{A Dirichlet prior helps with a small public sample.} With 200 instead of
  2,000 public records, accuracy at $m=10$ drops from 69.4\% (a rerun on the same seeds)
  to 65.1\%, and the confidence becomes 8.6 points overconfident
  (Figure~\ref{fig:confidence} right). Per-seed calibration error rises from 3.2 to
  8.6 points ($p=4\times10^{-5}$). The Dirichlet prior removes most of this: overconfidence drops
  from 8.6, 7.1 and 5.9 points to 1.6, 2.0 and 1.7 at $m=10$, 25 and 50
  ($p\leq3\times10^{-5}$ each), while accuracy and AUROC show no measurable change ($p\geq0.19$). It
  vouches for fewer rows (24\% against 33\% at $m=10$) but has higher precision on those rows
  (94.8\% against 88.7\%). With 2,000 records it has no measurable effect
  ($p\geq0.13$). In this setting, 200 public records suffice to bring mean confidence within about two points of accuracy.
