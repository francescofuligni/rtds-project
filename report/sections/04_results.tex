\subsection{More trees, and a little background knowledge (SQ1)}
\label{sec:knowledge}
The number of released trees drives the leakage (Figure~\ref{fig:attack},
Table~\ref{tab:attack}). Without background knowledge the error falls from 34.6\% at
$m=1$ to 25.3\% at $m=200$ ($-9.4$ points, $p=2\times10^{-11}$). At $m=1$ the attack is
no better than always guessing male: a single tree constrains few cells, and the solver
tends to fill the free sex cells with 0, which encodes male. For half of the seeds the
$m=1$ error equals the share of women in the sample exactly. Against the majority guess
it gains 7.7 points at $m=200$ ($p=5\times10^{-11}$); the uniform and frequency
baselines would make it look strong from the start.

Background knowledge adds about one point on top. Averaged over $m$, marital status with
education lowers the error by 1.3 points, and with age by 0.9 points. Single attributes
give at most 0.5 points, and age alone gives nothing measurable. Education is the more
useful second attribute: its gain exceeds that of age by 0.5 points ($p=0.03$,
uncorrected). Tested at each $m$ separately, none of the 42 tests per run survives Holm
correction. Two checks explain why these effects are hard to measure. First, the solver
adds noise of the same size: None is the same problem in both runs, yet 15\% of its
(seed, $m$) cells returned a different error (s.d.\ 2.0 points), because CP-SAT's
parallel workers race and any consistent table is accepted. Second, the mechanism we
expected, that knowing A helps when trees split on sex and A on the same path, is not
supported: the share of such trees does not predict the gain ($|r|\leq0.03$, all
$p>0.2$).

\subsection{Which trees are released (SQ2)}
\label{sec:selection}
The choice of trees matters more than background knowledge. With marital status and
education known, random releases reach 24.3\% error at $m=200$, against 30.4\% for the
first 200 trees in enumeration order ($-6.2$ points, $p=8\times10^{-11}$), and the
curves separate from about $m=10$ ($-2.8$ points, $p=1\times10^{-4}$). In a spot check
on three seeds, the first 10 trees shared 21--33\% of their leaves pairwise (Jaccard
index) and had 21--24 distinct leaves, against 3--4\% and 53--83 for 10 random trees:
consecutive trees are near-copies. The confidence attack shows the same effect: at
$m=25$ its accuracy is 61.7\% with the first trees and 73.9\% with random ones
($p=9\times10^{-5}$, 20 seeds).

\subsection{Which records the attacker can trust (SQ3)}
\paragraph{Counting reconstructions is not a confidence.} Scoring a record by how often
  it comes back fails. At $m=10$, the share of single-tree reconstructions containing a
  record gives claims that are 6.6\% correct at a mean stated confidence of 49\%;
  agreement across solver seeds gives 34\% correct at 86\% stated. The reconstructions
  agree because CP-SAT fills free cells the same way, not because the release forces
  those values.

\paragraph{Prior-guided sampling is calibrated without fitting.} Its stated confidence
  exceeds its accuracy by only 0.7 to 2.3 points (Table~\ref{tab:confidence},
  Figure~\ref{fig:confidence} left). Its per-seed calibration error
  $|\text{confidence}-\text{accuracy}|$ matches that of the public-statistics baseline
  ($p\geq0.17$; smaller at $m=50$, $p=0.02$). It is also more accurate, 69.2\% at $m=10$
  and 77.8\% at $m=50$ against 58.5\% without trees ($p\leq2\times10^{-4}$ at every $m$;
  all 20 seeds better at $m=25$ and 50), and it ranks claims better (AUROC 0.79--0.83
  against 0.74).

\paragraph{The leak is concentrated in records the attacker can name.} At $m=50$ the
  attacker vouches for 45\% of the private rows, and 97.6\% of those are correct; public
  statistics alone vouch for 10\%. Claims at confidence 1.0 (all 20 samples agree) were
  wrong in 2 of 673 cases. A global accuracy of 78\% hides that about half of the
  records are reconstructed almost certainly, and that the attacker knows which half.

\paragraph{Little public data suffices with a Dirichlet prior.} With 200 instead of
  2,000 public records, accuracy at $m=10$ drops from 69.4\% (a rerun on the same seeds)
  to 65.1\%, and the confidence becomes 8.6 points overconfident
  (Figure~\ref{fig:confidence} right; per-seed calibration error 3.2 against 8.6 points,
  $p=4\times10^{-5}$). The Dirichlet prior removes most of this: overconfidence drops
  from 8.6, 7.1 and 5.9 points to 1.6, 2.0 and 1.7 at $m=10$, 25 and 50
  ($p\leq2\times10^{-5}$ each), while accuracy and AUROC do not change ($p\geq0.19$). It
  vouches for fewer rows (24\% against 33\% at $m=10$) but is right on more of them
  (94.8\% against 88.7\%). With 2,000 records it changes nothing measurable
  ($p\geq0.25$). A calibrated confidence thus needs only about 200 public records (0.7\%
  of the population).
