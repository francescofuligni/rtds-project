\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Background knowledge of $A$ and/or $B$ significantly reduces reconstruction error on $T$}

Figure~\ref{fig:error-vs-m} shows the mean reconstruction error on $T$ across 200 repetitions, for each background-knowledge condition and number of released trees $m$, against the two baselines (means taken over all repetitions, since the baselines do not depend on $m$).

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/reconstruction_error_vs_m.pdf}
  \caption{Reconstruction error on $T=\texttt{sex}$ vs.\ number of released trees $m$, by background-knowledge condition. Error bars are $\pm 1$ standard error of the mean over 200 repetitions (fewer at $m=50,100$ where some repetitions' Rashomon sets or solves did not reach that point -- see Limitations).}
  \label{fig:error-vs-m}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{The released trees leak substantially more than either baseline.} All four conditions solve to error between $0.31$ and $0.36$ across every $m$ tested, against a uniform-random baseline of $0.50$ and a holdout-marginal baseline of $0.44$.

\paragraph{A clean ordering: $A{+}B < A \approx B < \text{None}$.} The four conditions separate cleanly and consistently from $m=10$ onward (Table~\ref{tab:means}), with $A{+}B$ giving the lowest error at every $m$ tested.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lccccc}
\toprule
 & $m=5$ & $m=10$ & $m=25$ & $m=50$ & $m=100$ \\
\midrule
None & 0.345 & 0.355 & 0.349 & 0.346 & 0.330 \\
$A$    & 0.342 & 0.345 & 0.341 & 0.336 & 0.323 \\
$B$    & 0.338 & 0.342 & 0.338 & 0.331 & 0.323 \\
$A{+}B$ & 0.332 & 0.331 & 0.325 & 0.317 & 0.315 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Mean reconstruction error on $T$ by condition and $m$, over 200 repetitions.}
\label{tab:means}
\end{table}

A paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test (same seed and $m$, condition vs.\ \emph{None}), pooled across all $m$, confirms this is not noise:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
 & $A$ & $B$ & $A{+}B$ \\
\midrule
mean difference vs.\ None & $-0.0077$ & $-0.0110$ & $-0.0211$ \\
$p$-value (pooled) & $6\times10^{-4}$ & $3\times10^{-7}$ & $1\times10^{-22}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Because this pools 15 tests (3 conditions $\times$5 values of $m$), we additionally report per-$m$ tests with Holm-Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons ($\alpha=0.05$): $A{+}B$ survives correction at $m=5,10,25,50$ (4 of 5 values; at $m=100$ it narrowly fails, $p=0.0097$, just short of its step-down threshold), and $B$ survives at $m=10$. $A$ alone does not survive the conservative per-$m$ correction at any single $m$, despite being significant pooled -- its effect size per cell ($\approx 0.008$--$0.01$) is simply too small relative to per-cell noise ($n=200$ per cell) to clear a corrected threshold alone, even though it reliably accumulates to significance when pooled. We read the overall pattern as: knowing \emph{both} $A$ and $B$ gives a robust, individually-significant effect; knowing either one alone gives a real but smaller effect that is only reliably detectable in aggregate. $B$ (education) is directionally the noisier of the two single-attribute conditions, consistent with a weaker empirical association with \texttt{sex} than marital-status has in this data.

\subsection{Testing the hypothesized mechanism: it does not hold up}

An earlier pass of this experiment (fewer, shallower trees) found no condition separation, and we hypothesized that background knowledge only helps where a released tree's root-to-leaf path jointly splits on $T$ \emph{and} $A$ or $B$ together -- deeper trees seemed to confirm this indirectly, since they both showed much more joint splitting and the first clear condition separation.

We tested this directly rather than leaving it as a plausible story. For every (seed, $m$) cell we computed the fraction of released trees containing at least one leaf whose path uses both $T$ and the attribute in question, and correlated it against that cell's observed error reduction (\emph{None} error minus the condition's error). Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism} shows the result: no relationship. Both attributes show a flat scatter (Spearman $\rho=0.03$, $p=0.42$, $n=949$ for $A$; $\rho=0.04$, $p=0.22$, $n=950$ for $B$).

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/mechanism_joint_split_correlation.pdf}
  \caption{Per-(seed, $m$) error reduction vs.\ fraction of released trees with a joint $T$+attribute split leaf. No correlation is visible for either attribute.}
  \label{fig:mechanism}
\end{figure}

We take this as a genuine negative result, not a nuisance to explain away: the simple structural story ("more joint splits $\Rightarrow$ more leakage") does not predict cell-to-cell variation, even though the aggregate effect across hundreds of cells is unambiguous (previous subsection). The most likely reading is that single-cell reconstruction error is dominated by solver and sample idiosyncrasies that swamp this particular structural signal, so the background-knowledge benefit is real on average but not well explained by this one coarse proxy -- a deeper mechanism account (e.g.\ weighting joint-split leaves by how many training rows they actually capture, rather than a binary per-tree indicator) is left for future work.

\subsection{Per-row confidence: solver agreement predicts correctness}
\label{sec:results-confidence}

No reviewed paper reports a confidence value for an individual reconstructed record from an ensemble of released models (gap G1 identified in the literature review); the solver-agreement proxy described in Section~\ref{sec:method-confidence} is our attempt to fill that gap. Figure~\ref{fig:calibration} bins the 20-seed, $m\in\{10,100\}$ sample by agreement rate and plots each bin's empirical accuracy.

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/calibration_reliability.pdf}
  \caption{Empirical accuracy vs.\ agreement rate across 15 repeated solves (confidence proxy), by $m$. Point size is proportional to bin count. The dashed line is perfect calibration (accuracy = agreement).}
  \label{fig:calibration}
\end{figure}

With $N_{\text{repeats}}=15$, agreement rate takes 8 well-populated values per $m$ (roughly $8/15$ through $15/15$; a handful of additional fractions appear from the rare cell where fewer than 15 repeats solved within the timeout, each with only a few rows -- see Limitations). The trend is upward but not strictly monotonic bin-to-bin: at $m=10$, accuracy moves from $0.57$ at agreement $8/15$ to a peak of $0.70$ at $14/15$ before settling at $0.68$ in the dominant full-agreement bin ($15/15$, $n=6{,}827$); at $m=100$ it rises from $0.50$ at $8/15$ to $0.80$ at $14/15$, then drops back to $0.70$ at $15/15$ ($n=5{,}768$) -- so even the best-populated bin is not the most accurate one. Row-level Spearman correlation between agreement and correctness is positive and highly significant at both, though modest in magnitude: $\rho=0.06$ ($p=4.8\times10^{-8}$, $n=8{,}000$) at $m=10$, and $\rho=0.08$ ($p=9.1\times10^{-13}$, $n=7{,}700$) at $m=100$.

This is a real, useful confidence signal -- an attacker (or an auditor) can trust a unanimous reconstruction meaningfully more than a contested one -- but it is not a \emph{calibrated probability}: every curve in Figure~\ref{fig:calibration} sits below the perfect-calibration line, so a row with full agreement across all 15 solves is correct about 68--70\% of the time, not close to 100\%. The agreement rate should be read as a valid relative ranking of confidence, not a literal probability of correctness.

\subsection{From raw agreement to an actual calibrated probability}

The previous subsection established correlation, not calibration, and did not separate the data used to observe the relationship from data used to evaluate it. We close that gap directly: split the 20 seeds into 14 training and 6 held-out test seeds, fit an isotonic regression mapping agreement rate to $P(\text{correct})$ on the training seeds only (separately for $m=10$ and $m=100$), and evaluate strictly on the 6 test seeds the mapping never saw.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
$m$ & raw Brier & calibrated Brier & raw ECE & calibrated ECE \\
\midrule
10  & 0.320 & 0.221 & 0.310 & 0.016 \\
100 & 0.331 & 0.231 & 0.318 & 0.072 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Calibration quality on held-out test seeds: raw agreement rate used directly as a probability, vs.\ the isotonic-calibrated probability fit on the disjoint training seeds. Lower is better for both metrics.}
\label{tab:calibration-quality}
\end{table}

The calibrated probability roughly halves the Brier score and cuts expected calibration error by a factor of 4--20$\times$ (Table~\ref{tab:calibration-quality}), evaluated entirely out-of-sample. Figure~\ref{fig:calibration-isotonic} shows why: the isotonic curve (fit on training seeds) tracks the held-out test points far more closely than the raw agreement value would, which -- used directly as a probability -- is systematically overconfident across the whole range.

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/calibration_isotonic.pdf}
  \caption{Isotonic-calibrated probability (fit on 14 training seeds) vs.\ raw agreement rate, both evaluated against empirical accuracy on 6 held-out test seeds never used to fit the calibration.}
  \label{fig:calibration-isotonic}
\end{figure}

This is, to our knowledge, the first attempt in this literature to produce an actual calibrated, validated per-record confidence value for a reconstruction attack against an ensemble of released models (as opposed to either no confidence measure at all, or an uncalibrated single-model entropy measure). We initially ran this with $N_{\text{repeats}}=7$ (four well-populated agreement levels) and asked whether a finer-grained proxy would improve the calibration; doubling to $N_{\text{repeats}}=15$ (eight levels, Table~\ref{tab:calibration-quality}) left Brier score and ECE essentially unchanged on held-out seeds. Agreement-level resolution was therefore not the bottleneck -- the limiting factor is more likely the held-out test-set size (6 seeds), not the coarseness of the confidence scale.

\paragraph{Limitations.} (i) The pooled Wilcoxon tests treat each (seed, $m$) pair as an independent observation; the five $m$-values drawn from the same seed reuse the same 100-row sample and overlapping trees from the same Rashomon set, so pooled $p$-values are a convenient summary rather than a fully independent combined test -- the per-$m$, Holm-corrected tests are the safer basis for any individual claim. (ii) At $m=100$, the \emph{None} condition most often timed out (CP-SAT returned \texttt{UNKNOWN} within the 120s budget); cell sizes shrink from 200 at $m=5$ to 156 at $m=100$ accordingly, and a handful of repetitions had Rashomon sets smaller than 50/100 trees and were excluded at those $m$ values entirely. (iii) A handful of odd-fraction agreement values (e.g.\ $0.50$, $0.69$, $0.85$ at $m=100$) come from the rare cell where fewer than 15 repeats solved within the timeout and have only 1--5 rows each; the reported trend is supported by the eight well-populated $k/15$ bins (hundreds to thousands of rows each), and even among those the relationship is non-monotonic bin-to-bin, not a clean staircase. (iv) The curated 13-column feature set and single enumeration-order tree selection (no diversity-aware strategy) remain as before. (v) The held-out calibration test set is only 6 seeds ($n\approx2{,}300$--$2{,}400$ rows); doubling the agreement-level resolution from four to eight levels (reported above) did not improve held-out Brier/ECE, so the test-set size -- not the confidence scale's granularity -- is the more likely remaining bottleneck before treating the calibrated probabilities as precise.
