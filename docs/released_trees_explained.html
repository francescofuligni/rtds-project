The story in one minute

Companies and governments publish small decision trees because people can read them. A published tree usually comes with leaf counts: how many training people ended up in each leaf, and what their outcome was. Those counts are facts about the private training data.

For one dataset there are often thousands of trees that are almost equally good. This collection is called the Rashomon set. Publishing several of them gives an attacker many independent sets of counts about the same people.

An attacker can treat the counts as a puzzle: find a table of people that matches every count. Earlier work showed this rebuilds much of the training data. But the attacker gets one table and no way to know which of its rows are real.

Our contribution: the attacker solves the puzzle 20 times, each time nudged towards a different plausible guess drawn from public data. Rows that come back in every solution are forced by the trees. Rows that change between solutions are guesses. The share of solutions that contain a row becomes that row's confidence, and it turns out to be honest without any tuning.

79%of the 100 private rows rebuilt exactly with 100 released trees (58.5% from public data alone)
45%of rows the attacker vouches for at ≥ 90% confidence with 50 trees; 98% of them are right
±2 ptsgap between stated confidence and real accuracy, with no fitting
1.3 ptsextra drop in error from knowing marital status and education; more trees give 9
Say it like this“When you publish many good decision trees, an attacker can rebuild a large part of the training data. And, new in our work, the attacker also knows which rebuilt people are real, with confidences that are honest without ever seeing the true data.”
Building block 1

The data: a table of people

We use UCI Adult, a standard dataset from the 1994 US census. After dropping rows with missing values it has 30,162 people. Each person has demographic attributes and a label: does this person earn more than $50,000 a year? 24.9% do, and 32.4% of the people are women.

The tree-building software we use (TreeFARMS) only works with yes/no columns, so every attribute becomes yes/no columns. We kept a small, readable set of 13:

2010never marriedhigh school1no
3101divorcedsome college1no

Three made-up rows to show the shape. Marital status has 4 groups (married, never married, divorced, widowed or separated) and education 5 (below high school, high school, some college, bachelor's, graduate), each written as one yes/no column per group. That makes 3 + 4 + 5 + 1 = 13 columns, plus the label.

In every experiment we draw a private table of 100 people. That table is what the model is trained on, and what the attacker tries to rebuild. A different random draw is called a seed; we repeat everything for 20 or 100 seeds so that no result depends on one lucky draw.

A useful fact for later: with these columns there are only 640 different possible people (combinations of values including the label). So two real people can be identical on paper, and a rebuilt row is "a record" that may appear several times.

Building block 2

What a released tree gives away

A decision tree asks yes/no questions and ends in leaves. The prediction in a leaf is the majority label of the training people who landed there. Published trees typically also show how many training people landed in each leaf, split by label. That is what we call the leaf counts.

An illustrative tree over 100 people (the numbers are made up but add up: 25 earn more than 50K, 75 do not).

One tree only says something about the few columns it asks about. Here it says nothing about sex or age. But each leaf count is an exact equation: "the number of training rows with married = yes, degree = yes and income > 50K is 10". This is the threat model of Ferry et al. (2024), whose attack DRAFT we reimplemented.

Why we assume the counts are publicInterpretable-model tools show leaf counts because they explain the model ("this rule covers 15 people, 10 of whom earn more"). Transparency rules push in the same direction. We also assume the attacker knows each row's income label; every paper in this line of work does, because the released model predicts it and the counts per label reveal the totals anyway.
Building block 3

Why counts leak: a toy puzzle

Four private people, two yes/no columns (female? married?), and their labels. The attacker sees only the labels and two released trees.

Combine them. Both high earners are male (tree 1) and married (tree 2): rows A and B are fully determined. The low earners contain one woman and one man, and one married and one single person. Two tables fit:

This tiny example contains the whole project:

  • The trees pin down some rows exactly (A and B). That is the leak.
  • Other rows stay ambiguous (C and D). A solver returns one of the two tables, and the attacker cannot tell which rows are pinned and which are a coin flip.
  • If the attacker knows from public statistics that low-earning women are more often single, table 2 is more likely. Combining "what the trees force" with "what is typical in the population" is exactly our method.
Building block 4

The Rashomon set: many equally good trees

For most datasets there is no single best tree. Many different trees score almost the same. This is called the Rashomon effect, after the film in which witnesses tell different but equally believable stories. The Rashomon set is the collection of all trees whose score is within a small margin of the best.

TreeFARMS (Xin et al., NeurIPS 2022) lists every tree in the Rashomon set. We use its settings as given in the project: each extra leaf costs 1% of accuracy (regularisation 0.01), and the set keeps every tree within 10% of the best score. On our 100-row tables the set held between 28 and about 5 million trees, typically around 6,000.

Why would anyone publish several trees? Researchers argue the Rashomon set is useful: it shows which conclusions hold in every good model, lets a user pick a tree they find fair or sensible, and supports variable-importance studies. Hsu et al. (ICML 2026) call it double-edged for exactly the reason we study: every extra released tree is another set of equations about the same people.

We release m trees, and m is the main dial in every experiment.

Building block 5

The attacker's solver: a Sudoku for tables

The attacker writes the private table as unknowns: 100 rows × 13 yes/no cells. Every leaf count of every released tree becomes a rule that the unknowns must satisfy. A constraint solver (Google OR-Tools CP-SAT) then searches for a table that obeys every rule at once, just as a Sudoku solver fills a grid that obeys every row, column and box rule.

  • A rule can also fix known values. That is how we give the attacker background knowledge: in condition A it is told everyone's marital status, so those cells are fixed before solving.
  • The solver can also be asked for the fitting table that is closest to a given guess, counted in differing cells. We use this for our method.
  • Each solve has a time limit (180 s in the main experiment, 30 s per sample in the confidence experiment). The solver runs several search strategies in parallel; with a single strategy it often found no table in time.
Why only 100 peopleSolving gets much harder with more rows. In our tests, 1,000 rows found no table within 180 s even with 10 trees. Earlier papers also use small tables (100 to 1,500 rows). So our results are about small tables; this is a stated limit.
Our method

Our idea: a confidence for every rebuilt row

The problem with a single answer

The solver returns one fitting table. In the toy puzzle that is a coin flip for rows C and D. In the real problem it is worse: cells that no tree constrains get filled with the solver's default value, which is 0. For the column "female", 0 means male. So a single table quietly calls many people male simply because nothing contradicted it.

Earlier work reports one number for the whole table (the share of wrong cells or rows). That tells a reader how good the attack is on average. It does not tell the attacker which rows to believe, and that is what an attacker needs to harm a particular person.

Prior-guided sampling, step by step

STEP 1Draw a plausible guessThe attacker starts from 100 empty rows of which only the income is known. For each row, pick a random public person with that income. There is no earlier solver run.
STEP 2Find the closest table that fitsAsk the solver for the table that satisfies every leaf count of all m trees and differs from the guess in as few cells as possible.
STEP 3Repeat 20 timesOnly the guess is new each time; the trees and the public data stay the same. The result is 20 fitting tables, all equally valid.
STEP 4VoteA record's confidence is the share of the 20 tables that contain it (at least k copies of it, see below). The best-scoring records fill the 100 output rows.

The public data are 2,000 other Adult records, never the private ones. This models an attacker with access to census-style data about the same population.

married man, degree, >50K100%
single woman, high school, ≤50K65%

Illustration. Each dot is one of the 20 fitting tables; a filled dot means that table contains the row.

Why this should give honest confidences

Think of the cells in two kinds:

  • Cells the trees force. Every fitting table must have them, whatever the guess. Rows built from them appear in all 20 tables: confidence close to 100%, and they really are right.
  • Cells the trees leave free. The solver keeps them as close to the random guess as it can, so they follow the guess. Because the guesses come from the real population, a free row appears about as often as such people are common in the population. That is exactly the right probability for "this row is in the private table".

So the confidence mixes "forced by the evidence" with "typical in the population" in the right proportions, without ever looking at the true private table. In statistics this trick is known as perturb-and-MAP: randomise the starting point, solve, and count, to approximate a probability over all fitting tables.

Why we expected it to workThe two simpler ideas we tried first fail for a clear reason. Solving each tree separately and counting agreement gives agreement because every solve uses the same default (male), not because of evidence. Changing only the solver's random seed has the same problem. Drawing the guess from public data replaces the arbitrary default with a realistic one, and the counting then measures something real.

From counts to the attacker's final table

Rows can repeat, so the attacker makes claims of the form "the private table contains at least k copies of this record". A claim's confidence is the share of the 20 tables with at least k copies. For each income label the attacker keeps as many highest-scoring claims as there are rows with that label (the labels are known), giving a 100-row table with a confidence on every row.

claim about record Xtable 1 (2 copies of X)table 2 (3 copies of X)confidence
at least 1 copyyesyes2/2 = 1.00
at least 2 copiesyesyes2/2 = 1.00
at least 3 copiesnoyes1/2 = 0.50

Shown with 2 tables instead of 20 to keep it small. Each claim competes for an output row on its own. A third copy can never score higher than the second, so the output never claims a third copy without the first two.

The private 100 are our ground truth: we only use them at the end to check which rows the attacker got right.”
Evaluation

How we measured

The attacker never sees the private table. We, the evaluators, do, and we use it only afterwards to score the attack.

  • Correct row: a claim "at least k copies of record r" is correct if the private table really has at least k copies of r. A record counts only if all 13 columns and the label match. This is strict: one wrong cell makes the row wrong.
  • Accuracy: the share of the 100 output rows that are correct.
  • Calibration: group rows by stated confidence and check the real accuracy in each group. Among rows stated at 80%, about 80% should be right. Our summary number is stated confidence minus accuracy (positive = overconfident).
  • Vouched rows: rows with confidence ≥ 90%: the ones an attacker would act on. We report how many there are and how many of them are right.
  • Baseline: the same attacker without any trees, using public statistics only (how common each record is). Anything above the baseline is what the trees leak.
  • Seeds and pairing: 20 seeds for the confidence experiments, 100 for the main one. Settings are compared on the same seeds (the same private table and the same released trees), so the comparison is "same people, different setting".
  • Statistics: the Wilcoxon signed-rank test compares two settings seed by seed without assuming normal data. Bands and error bars are 95% intervals over seeds.

The main experiment uses a different error

Francesco's original question measures error on one sensitive column, sex. The rebuilt table's rows come in arbitrary order, so we first pair every rebuilt row with the most similar real row (ignoring sex, so the evaluator does not leak the answer), then count how many paired rows have the wrong sex. Guessing everyone male would score 0.32 (the share of women); guessing randomly from population shares scores 0.445.

The five results

1 · The trees leak records

Releasing trees adds 11 to 20 points of accuracy over public statistics alone, better on 19 or 20 of the 20 seeds. The bigger change is in the rows the attacker can trust: public statistics let it vouch for 10% of the table; 50 trees let it vouch for 45%, and 98% of those are right. Accuracy flattens after about 50 trees.

Say it like this“Without the trees, the attacker could name one person in ten with confidence. With 50 trees it can name almost half of the table, and it is right 98% of the time.”

58.5% may look high for “no trees”. With only 640 possible records, common profiles (say, a married man with a high-school education earning ≤50K) are easy to guess. The leak is the difference to this baseline. At m = 100, two of 20 seeds produced no table in time; they were the easiest seeds at m = 50, so dropping them lowers the average rather than flattering it.

2 · The attacker knows which rows are right

Points on the dashed diagonal would be perfectly honest. Our attack (blue) stays close to it at every confidence level, and its stated confidence exceeds its real accuracy by only 0.7 to 2.3 points across m. Nothing was fitted or tuned on true data to get this. Rows at confidence 100% are right 97.5 to 99.7% of the time.

Say it like this“If the attack says it is 80% sure about a row, it is right about 80% of the time. The attacker gets this for free: it never needs the true data to check itself.”

3 · Background knowledge helps a little; more trees help much more

This is Francesco's original research question. Does knowing marital status (A) and education or age (B) for everyone help the attacker find their sex? We tested four conditions (none, A, B, A+B) on 14 tree counts from 1 to 200, 100 seeds, with education and age run on the same seeds and trees.

known to the attackererror drop with B = educationwith B = age
A: marital status0.6 points (p = 0.02)0.6 points (p = 0.006)
B alone0.6 points (p = 0.0002)0.1 points (p = 0.42)
A + B1.3 points (p = 1.5·10⁻¹³)0.9 points (p = 2·10⁻⁶)
  • The effect is real and replicates. Francesco's earlier run found 2.0 points for A+B; it was done with the first m trees, which carry less information. With random trees the trees themselves explain more, leaving less for background knowledge: 1.3 points.
  • The trees matter much more: going from 1 to 200 trees lowers the error from 0.35 to 0.25.
  • Education helps more than age, but the difference is significant at only a few tree counts.
  • The mechanism we expected is not supported. We guessed that knowledge helps where a tree splits on both sex and the known attribute. The share of such splits does not predict the gain (correlation about −0.03).
Say it like this“Knowing two extra attributes for everyone helps, reliably but modestly: about one point. Releasing more trees helps the attacker nine times as much.”

4 · Which trees are released matters

TreeFARMS lists its trees like an odometer: tree 1, 2, 3, … differ only in their last branch, the way 1000, 1001, 1002 differ only in the last digit. So the first m trees are near-copies and say almost the same thing.

Illustration of the order. Releasing m random trees from the list gives m genuinely different views of the data.

Which experiment this is: Hendrik's record-confidence experiment, the prior-guided attack with 2,000 public records, 20 seeds, measured as rows correct. Grey is the earlier run, which released the first m trees. Blue is the final run, which releases m random trees, on the same seeds. First 10 or 25 trees: 60.8% and 61.7% correct. Random 10 or 25 trees: 69.2% and 73.9%, better by 8 to 12 points (p ≤ 0.0002).

Francesco's experiment shows the same effect in its own measure. His earlier run used the first m trees, and knowing A + B lowered the sex error by 2.0 points. The final run with random trees finds 1.3 points (result 3): random trees already tell the attacker more, so the extra knowledge adds less. Every final result in this guide and on the poster uses random trees; the first-m runs only show how much that choice matters.

5 · With little public data, a Dirichlet prior keeps the confidences honest

What the figure shows: Hendrik's record-confidence experiment again (random trees, 20 seeds), but the attacker gets only 200 public records instead of 2,000. Bars are overconfidence: stated confidence minus real accuracy, so 0 is perfectly honest. Grey is the plain attack: our normal prior-guided attack, with trees, drawing each guess straight from the public records. Blue is the same attack, with the same trees, plus the Dirichlet step. Neither is the no-trees baseline.

The problem. Result 2 assumes the attacker has 2,000 public records. The plain method treats the public people as if they were the whole population: every guess is drawn from exactly those people. With only 200, that goes wrong in two ways. A kind of person who happens not to be among the 200 never appears in any guess. And all 20 guesses lean the same way, the way those 200 happen to lean. The free cells then come out the same in many of the 20 tables, the votes agree, and the attack sounds surer than it is: 6 to 9 points overconfident. (With only per-attribute percentages and no records at all, it is 5 points overconfident.) The leak itself remains; only the honesty of the confidence suffers.

The fix. Before each guess, first imagine one plausible population, then draw the guess from that population. A population here is a share for each of the 640 kinds of person. The Dirichlet distribution is the standard tool for drawing such a set of shares from counts: the drawn shares sit near what was observed, and spread wider when the counts are small. Every kind of person also gets a small head start of 0.1 counts, so kinds missing from the 200 can still turn up now and then. The next sample imagines a new population.

Real draws for one kind of person among the not-rich public people. The shaded band holds 90% of all possible draws. With 200 records the attacker honestly does not know whether this kind of person is 4% or 10% of the population, so its 20 guesses disagree more and its confidences come out lower. With 2,000 records the draws barely move, so the method behaves like the plain one.

What the attacker needs extra: no data, one assumptionThe input is the same 200 public records: only their counts per kind of person go in. Each draw gives every kind a random number centred on its count (wobbling by about the square root of the count), then divides by the total. So kinds seen often barely move and kinds seen rarely move a lot. This adds no information, which is why accuracy does not change; it only removes false certainty. What it does add is one assumption: the 0.1 head start, meaning a kind never seen still has a small chance. That value is the smoothing chosen earlier for the public-statistics baseline by checking it against private tables; it was not re-tuned for the Dirichlet step. Say so if asked.

The result. With 200 records, overconfidence drops from 6–9 points to about 2, with the same accuracy. The attacker vouches for fewer rows (34% instead of 43% at m = 25) but is right on more of them (96% instead of 93%). With 2,000 records the Dirichlet step changes nothing, so it is a safe default.

Say it like this“If the attacker has little public data, it should also be unsure about the population itself. Building that uncertainty in keeps the confidences honest.”
Be upfront

Limits to state openly

  • Small tables. 100 private rows and 13 yes/no columns. The solver does not scale to 1,000 rows in our time limits.
  • The attacker knows each row's income label, as in all related work.
  • Public data from the same population. Tested with 2,000 records, 200 records and percentages only; data from a different population is untested.
  • Solver time limits. In some confidence runs many of the 20 samples hit the 30 s limit and returned the best table found rather than the proven closest one. We checked this: with twice the threads and a 60 s limit, more samples reached the proven optimum (62% to 71%) and accuracy, calibration and vouched rows did not change (68.4% vs 68.5% correct).
  • The solver is not perfectly repeatable. With several parallel strategies, the same problem can return a different but equally valid table. In the main experiment this changed about 15% of cells' errors between identical runs; it averages out but adds noise.
  • Missing values. Rows with any missing value were dropped (32,561 to 30,162), so the population is the complete-case part of Adult.
Preparation

Questions to expect

What exactly is new compared to Ferry et al.?

Ferry et al. (2024) rebuild the training table of a random forest with a constraint solver (DRAFT); a companion paper (Ferry et al., SaTML 2024) builds a probabilistic reconstruction from interpretable models. Both report how good the reconstruction is overall. We give the attacker a confidence for each row that is calibrated without ground truth, and we study a released Rashomon set rather than one model. Dick et al. (PNAS 2023) rank reconstructed census records by confidence from published statistics; we bring that idea to released trees.

Why is a confidence per row such a big deal?

An attack is dangerous when it lets someone act on a particular person. An average error of 25% says nothing about which rows are among the good 75%. With honest confidences the attacker can keep only the rows it is 90% sure of, and almost all of those are right.

How can it be calibrated without ground truth? Didn't you use the true data?

The attacker never uses the true data: the confidence comes only from the released trees and public records. We use the true data afterwards, as evaluators, to check that the confidences were honest. They were, within about 2 points.

Isn't 79% accuracy mostly the public data?

Public statistics alone give 58.5%. The trees add 11 to 20 points, and they more than quadruple the number of rows the attacker can name with 90% confidence (10% to 45%). The leak is that difference.

Why only 100 rows? Why not 1,000?

The constraint solver did not find any table for 1,000 rows within 180 s even with only 10 trees, and 200 trees at 1,000 rows needed more than 5 GB of memory per solve. Related papers use 100 to 1,500 rows. We state it as a limit.

Why sex as the sensitive column?

It was Francesco's design for the main experiment: a binary, sensitive attribute that is in every row. The record-confidence experiment does not single out a column: a row counts as correct only if all 13 columns match.

Why would leaf counts ever be published?

Interpretable-model tools show them to explain decisions, and transparency rules encourage publishing models. It is also the standard threat model of this research line. Defences include publishing fewer trees, adding noise to the counts (differential privacy), or not publishing counts.

Why does background knowledge help so little?

Two reasons we can support. First, the trees already pin down much of the table, and random trees carry more information than near-copies, so less is left to gain (2.0 points with the first m trees, 1.3 with random trees). Second, the solver noise between equally valid tables is about as large as the gain. Our guess that it works through trees that split on both sex and the known attribute was tested and not supported.

Why 20 samples, and why random trees?

20 samples give confidence steps of 5%, enough to tell 60% from 90%; doubling the samples changed nothing measurable in our ablation. Random trees because TreeFARMS lists near-copies first, which understates the leak by 8 to 12 points.

Why does accuracy flatten after about 50 trees?

Later trees mostly repeat questions earlier ones already asked, and some cells are never constrained by any tree in the set. Those cells can only be guessed from the population, whatever m is.

Why the Wilcoxon test?

Each seed gives a pair of numbers (setting 1 vs setting 2 on the same private table and trees). The Wilcoxon signed-rank test checks whether the differences lean consistently one way, without assuming they are normally distributed.

What would you do next?

Add differential-privacy noise to the leaf counts and see whether the confidences still hold; scale to larger tables; test public data from a different population; study which kinds of people are most exposed.
